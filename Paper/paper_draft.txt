SARGASSUM ALGAL BLOOMS AND HOUSEHOLD WELFARE IN THE DOMINICAN REPUBLIC

ABSTRACT

This paper estimates the effect of Sargassum seaweed inundation on
household income and employment in the Dominican Republic using a
monthly municipality-level panel from 2017 to 2025. Combining satellite
data on Sargassum intensity with quarterly household survey data from
86 municipalities, I find that Sargassum significantly reduces household
income during peak bloom months (May–September), with an estimated
effect of -64.69 log points (p < 0.05). Surprisingly, the effect is
largest in non-coastal municipalities, suggesting disruption to tourism
supply chains extends well beyond the shoreline. Among income groups,
the negative effect is largest for the poorest households, and becomes
detectable for the lowest-income earners when zero-income households
are excluded from the analysis. Results are
identified through municipality and year-by-month fixed effects, which
control for time-invariant geographic differences and all common
national shocks.

1. INTRODUCTION

The Dominican Republic is among the countries most severely affected
by the mass influx of pelagic Sargassum seaweed that has intensified
across the tropical Atlantic since approximately 2011. In years such as
2018, 2021, 2022, 2023, and 2025, enormous blooms washed ashore along
the country's Caribbean and Atlantic coastlines, fouling beaches,
generating toxic hydrogen sulphide gas as the seaweed decomposed, and
disrupting the marine environments on which coastal communities depend.
For a country where tourism accounts for roughly 8% of GDP and attracts
around eight million visitors annually — including the internationally
known Punta Cana resort corridor — repeated Sargassum inundation
represents a sustained economic threat.

Despite growing ecological and public health documentation of this
phenomenon, systematic evidence on its economic consequences at the
household level remains scarce. This paper helps fill that gap by
estimating the effect of Sargassum intensity on household income and
employment across Dominican municipalities from 2017 to 2025.

I combine two data sources. The first is satellite-derived monthly
Sargassum intensity data constructed from NASA's MODIS-Aqua sensor
using the Alternative Floating Algae Index (AFAI). The second is the
Encuesta Nacional Continua de Fuerza de Trabajo (ENCFT), the Dominican
Republic's quarterly household labour force and income survey. Merging
these at the municipality-month level produces a panel of 9,089
observations across 86 municipalities. I estimate the income effect
of Sargassum using OLS regression with municipality and year-by-month
fixed effects, which absorb permanent geographic differences and all
shocks common to all municipalities in a given month.

Three findings emerge. First, Sargassum significantly reduces household
income during peak bloom months (May–September), but the effect is not
detectable when averaged across the full year, suggesting the harm is
concentrated in the months when blooms are actually present. Second,
the peak-season effect is paradoxically stronger in non-coastal
municipalities than coastal ones, pointing to disruption of tourism
supply chains and regional labour markets that extend beyond the
immediate shoreline. Third, the income loss follows the expected
distributional pattern — largest for poorer households — though this
gradient only becomes statistically clear for the bottom group when
households reporting zero income are excluded.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 provides
background on Sargassum. Section 3 describes the data. Section 4
presents the empirical strategy. Section 5 reports results. Section 6
concludes.

2. BACKGROUND

Sargassum is a genus of brown macroalgae that forms vast floating mats
across the tropical Atlantic. What researchers now call the Great
Atlantic Sargassum Belt stretches from the west coast of Africa to the
Gulf of Mexico and the Caribbean. While Sargassum has always existed
in the Atlantic in moderate quantities — providing important habitat
for marine life — the volume of pelagic Sargassum has increased
dramatically since approximately 2011. Scientists attribute this to a
combination of elevated ocean surface temperatures, shifting Atlantic
circulation patterns, and increased nutrient runoff from deforestation
and intensive agriculture in West Africa and the Amazon basin.

When Sargassum washes ashore in large quantities, it decomposes and
releases hydrogen sulphide and ammonia, gases that are hazardous to
human health at high concentrations. Thick mats of rotting seaweed
make beaches inaccessible for tourism, entangle fishing gear, degrade
water quality, and can damage coral reef ecosystems. For Caribbean
tourism economies, the visual and olfactory impact is particularly
damaging: a beach covered in dark, decomposing seaweed cannot attract
the international visitors that coastal communities depend on.

The Dominican Republic's exposure is acute. Its coastline borders both
the Caribbean Sea and the Atlantic Ocean, and the prevailing currents
and winds bring Sargassum directly to its shores during the peak
season of May through September. While some coastal communities have
developed rudimentary collection and removal operations, the scale of
recent blooms has repeatedly overwhelmed local capacity.

3. DATA

3.1 Satellite Sargassum Data

I measure Sargassum exposure using the Alternative Floating Algae Index
(AFAI), a spectral index derived from NASA's MODIS-Aqua satellite that
detects floating vegetation at the ocean surface. Monthly AFAI data at
4km spatial resolution are sourced from NASA's ERDDAP data server,
covering the waters within 20km of the Dominican coastline from January
2017 to December 2025.

For each municipality–month observation, Sargassum intensity is
computed as:

    AFAI_sargassum = mean( max(AFAI − 0.001, 0) )

The 0.001 threshold removes background noise and low-signal pixels;
values above this threshold indicate detectable Sargassum. This measure
captures both the spatial coverage and density of Sargassum mats in
the coastal waters adjacent to each municipality.

Municipalities are classified as coastal (45 of 86 in the analysis
sample) if their administrative boundaries directly touch the ocean
shoreline. The Dominican Republic–Haiti land border is excluded from
this classification to avoid incorrectly treating border municipalities
as coastal. Non-coastal municipalities are assigned Sargassum exposure
based on ocean pixels within a 20km buffer, capturing regional offshore
exposure rather than direct shoreline contact.

3.2 Household Survey Data

Income and employment data come from the ENCFT, the Dominican
Republic's quarterly continuous labour force survey. The survey is
designed to be representative at the quarterly level and contains
detailed information on household income from wages and self-employment,
as well as individual employment status. I use survey waves from 2016
to 2025.

For each municipality-quarter, I compute mean household log income and
mean log income separately for each national income tertile group
(bottom, middle, and top thirds of the per-capita household income
distribution within each year). These tertile-specific income measures
allow me to test whether the Sargassum effect is larger for poorer
households.

Approximately 17.6% of surveyed households report zero income. As
discussed in Section 5, I construct a second set of tertiles that
excludes these households and use it as a robustness check.

3.3 Analysis Panel

I merge monthly satellite data with quarterly survey data by assigning
each month the income observation from the survey quarter that contains
it. The final panel contains 9,089 observations across 86
municipalities and the years 2017–2025.

4. EMPIRICAL STRATEGY

The main estimating equation is:

    log(income_it) = β · AFAI_it + α_i + γ_t + ε_it         (1)

where i indexes municipalities and t indexes year-month periods.
AFAI_it is the Sargassum intensity measure in municipality i in
period t. α_i are municipality fixed effects and γ_t are year-by-month
fixed effects. Standard errors are clustered at the municipality level.

The municipality fixed effects α_i absorb all time-invariant differences
across municipalities — permanent income differences, geographic
location, distance from major tourism centres, and baseline economic
structure. The year-by-month fixed effects γ_t absorb every shock that
affects all municipalities simultaneously in a given calendar month of
a given year, including the seasonal pattern of Sargassum itself,
national economic trends, and common weather events. After controlling
for both sets of fixed effects, identification relies entirely on
within-municipality variation in Sargassum intensity over time — that
is, months in which a specific municipality experienced more Sargassum
than was typical for that place at that time of year.

To test whether the effect is concentrated during the months when
Sargassum blooms are most intense, I estimate equation (1) on a peak
season subsample restricted to May through September:

    log(income_it) = β_peak · AFAI_it + α_i + γ_t + ε_it    (2)

To test for heterogeneous effects by income group, I replace the
dependent variable with mean log income for each tertile group k:

    log(income_Tk_it) = β_k · AFAI_it + α_i + γ_t + ε_it    (3)

where k ∈ {bottom, middle, top}. This tests whether the income response
differs across the distribution, with the prediction that poorer
households — who have fewer savings and less ability to absorb shocks —
should show larger negative coefficients.

I also estimate equations (1) and (2) separately for coastal and
non-coastal municipalities to test whether direct shoreline exposure
produces a larger effect than regional spillovers.

The key identifying assumption is that, conditional on municipality
and year-by-month fixed effects, no unobserved factor simultaneously
drives Sargassum intensity in a specific municipality and that
municipality's income within the same month. The most plausible threat
would be a concurrent local shock — such as a hurricane — that affects
both. I cannot formally rule this out, which is a limitation of the
analysis.

5. RESULTS

5.1 Main Results

Table 1 presents the main estimates. The full-year coefficient on
Sargassum intensity is negative (-5.71) but statistically
insignificant, suggesting no detectable average effect when averaged
across all months including those when Sargassum is largely absent.

The peak season result is markedly sharper. Restricting the sample to
May–September, when Sargassum blooms are most intense, yields a
coefficient of -64.69 (p < 0.05). A one-unit increase in mean excess
AFAI during peak months is associated with a 64.69 log-point reduction
in municipal household income. The contrast between the full-year and
peak-season estimates is consistent with Sargassum being the mechanism:
the income loss materialises specifically in the months when blooms are
present, not year-round.

5.2 Heterogeneity by Coastal Proximity

Table 3 presents results for coastal and non-coastal municipalities
separately. Over the full year, neither group shows a significant
effect. During peak season, the significant effect is found in
non-coastal municipalities (-70.87, p < 0.05), while coastal
municipalities show a negative but statistically insignificant
estimate (-58.95).

This finding is initially counterintuitive — one might expect direct
shoreline exposure to produce the largest impacts. Several explanations
are plausible. Coastal households tend to have more diversified income
sources, combining fishing, tourism employment, and local trade, which
may allow partial offsetting of Sargassum shocks when one income
source is disrupted. Non-coastal municipalities that supply tourism
services — accommodation, transport, food production — may face a
large demand-side collapse when Sargassum closes beaches, with fewer
alternative income streams. Coastal households may also have higher
baseline income variance year-round due to fishing seasonality, making
it statistically harder to isolate the Sargassum signal. Whatever the
mechanism, the finding suggests that the economic costs of Sargassum
extend substantially beyond the municipalities that physically border
the sea.

5.3 Heterogeneity by Income Tertile

Table 2 presents results by income tertile. Full-year coefficients
follow the expected gradient in absolute magnitude: bottom tertile
(-157.9), middle tertile (-20.62), top tertile (-11.04). This pattern
is consistent with lower-income households having less ability to insure
against environmental shocks through savings, credit access, or income
diversification.

None of the three groups reach conventional significance over the full
year. The bottom tertile's coefficient, though large, is extremely
imprecise (SE = 115.7). This occurs for a structural reason:
approximately 5% of municipality-month observations in the bottom
tertile group report zero household income. Log-transforming these
observations produces an outcome of zero regardless of Sargassum
intensity, which floors the dependent variable and inflates noise. The
standard deviation of log income in the bottom tertile is 1.93 compared
to 0.34 for the middle tertile, reflecting this problem directly.

During peak season (Table 2, columns 4–6), all coefficients are
negative: bottom (-117.6), middle (-41.86, p < 0.05), top (-48.76).
The middle tertile peak-season estimate is larger in absolute value than
its full-year counterpart (-41.86 vs -20.62), consistent with the effect
being concentrated in bloom months.

5.4 Robustness: Excluding Zero-Income Households

Table 4 addresses the zero-income floor problem by excluding the 17.6%
of surveyed households that report zero income and recomputing tertile
cutoffs on the remaining positive-income sample. The bottom tertile
in this non-zero sample (T1_nz) therefore represents the lowest-earning
workers in the Dominican Republic — not households with no income —
and has a higher income threshold: for example, the bottom/middle
boundary rises from RD$5,000 to RD$7,000 per capita per month in 2024.

The results change in an informative way. Full-year coefficients for
all three non-zero tertiles are negative but insignificant, reflecting
that the effect remains hard to detect over non-peak months. During
peak season, however, the bottom non-zero tertile produces a negative
and significant coefficient (-45.86, p < 0.05). The significance of
the bottom group in the non-zero sample — compared to its insignificance
in the original sample — confirms that the zero-income floor was masking
a real effect on low-income earners. The poorest workers do experience
significant income losses during peak Sargassum months; it is simply not
possible to detect this through the income measure for households that
report no income at all.

6. CONCLUSION

This paper provides causal evidence that Sargassum inundation reduces
household income in the Dominican Republic. The main finding is a
peak-season income effect of -64.69 log points (p < 0.05), identified
through within-municipality, within-time variation in Sargassum
intensity after controlling for municipality and year-by-month fixed
effects.

Two findings extend beyond this headline result. First, the significant
peak-season effect is found in non-coastal municipalities, suggesting
that Sargassum's economic damage propagates inland through disruption
to tourism supply chains and regional labour markets. Policy responses
focused exclusively on the immediate coastline may therefore
underestimate the true scope of the problem. Second, the income loss
is largest for the poorest households, but only becomes detectable
for the bottom group when zero-income households are excluded from the
analysis. This points to a broader measurement challenge: income-based
welfare measures cannot capture the impact on households with no formal
income, who may in fact be the most vulnerable to environmental shocks
operating through non-income channels such as food security, health
costs, or access to public services.

Limitations of this analysis include the use of quarterly rather than
monthly income data, the municipality-level aggregation of both the
treatment and outcome variables, and the inability to formally rule out
time-varying local confounders. Future work with finer spatial or
temporal resolution, health and nutrition outcomes, or a valid
instrumental variable could strengthen identification and provide a
more complete picture of Sargassum's welfare consequences in the
Caribbean.
