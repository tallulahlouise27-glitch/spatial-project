SARGASSUM ALGAL BLOOMS AND HOUSEHOLD WELFARE IN THE DOMINICAN REPUBLIC

ABSTRACT

This paper estimates the effect of Sargassum seaweed inundation on
household income and employment in the Dominican Republic using a
quarterly municipality-level panel from 2017 to 2025. Combining
satellite data on Sargassum intensity with household survey data from
86 municipalities, I find that Sargassum significantly reduces household
income during peak bloom quarters (Q2–Q3, April–September). A one-
standard-deviation increase in peak-season Sargassum intensity (0.61
milli-AFAI units) is associated with a 0.049 log-point (approximately
4.7%) reduction in mean municipal household income (p < 0.05). No
significant effect is detected on the employment rate, suggesting the
channel operates through earnings rather than job losses. The peak-
season income effect is larger in magnitude for non-coastal
municipalities than coastal ones, pointing to disruption of tourism
supply chains beyond the immediate shoreline. Results are identified
through municipality and year-by-quarter fixed effects, which absorb
time-invariant geographic differences and all common national shocks.

1. INTRODUCTION

The Dominican Republic is among the countries most severely affected
by the mass influx of pelagic Sargassum seaweed that has intensified
across the tropical Atlantic since approximately 2011. In years such as
2018, 2021, 2022, 2023, and 2025, enormous blooms washed ashore along
the country's Caribbean and Atlantic coastlines, fouling beaches,
generating toxic hydrogen sulphide gas as the seaweed decomposed, and
disrupting the marine environments on which coastal communities depend.
For a country where tourism accounts for roughly 8% of GDP and attracts
around eight million visitors annually — including the internationally
known Punta Cana resort corridor — repeated Sargassum inundation
represents a sustained economic threat.

Despite growing ecological and public health documentation of this
phenomenon, systematic evidence on its economic consequences at the
household level remains scarce. This paper helps fill that gap by
estimating the effect of Sargassum intensity on household income and
employment across Dominican municipalities from 2017 to 2025.

I combine two data sources. The first is satellite-derived Sargassum
intensity data constructed from NASA's MODIS-Aqua sensor using the
Alternative Floating Algae Index (AFAI). The second is the Encuesta
Nacional Continua de Fuerza de Trabajo (ENCFT), the Dominican
Republic's quarterly household labour force and income survey. Merging
these at the municipality-quarter level produces a panel of 3,095
observations across 86 municipalities. I estimate the income effect
of Sargassum using OLS regression with municipality and year-by-quarter
fixed effects, which absorb permanent geographic differences and all
shocks common to all municipalities in a given quarter.

Three findings emerge. First, Sargassum significantly reduces household
income during peak bloom quarters (Q2 and Q3, covering April through
September). A one-standard-deviation increase in peak-season Sargassum
intensity (0.61 milli-AFAI units) is associated with a 0.048 log-point
(approximately 4.7%) reduction in mean municipal household income
(p < 0.05). No significant effect is detected when the sample is
extended to all four quarters, consistent with the mechanism being
inactive when blooms are absent. Second, the employment rate shows no significant response,
suggesting that Sargassum reduces earnings conditional on employment —
through reduced hours, lower tips, or wage cuts in tourism-adjacent
sectors — rather than causing outright job losses. Third, the peak-
season income effect is larger in magnitude for non-coastal
municipalities than coastal ones, pointing to disruption of tourism
supply chains and regional labour markets that extend beyond the
immediate shoreline.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 provides
background on Sargassum. Section 3 describes the data. Section 4
presents the empirical strategy. Section 5 reports results. Section 6
concludes.

2. BACKGROUND

Sargassum is a genus of brown macroalgae that forms vast floating mats
across the tropical Atlantic. What researchers now call the Great
Atlantic Sargassum Belt stretches from the west coast of Africa to the
Gulf of Mexico and the Caribbean. While Sargassum has always existed
in the Atlantic in moderate quantities — providing important habitat
for marine life — the volume of pelagic Sargassum has increased
dramatically since approximately 2011. Scientists attribute this to a
combination of elevated ocean surface temperatures, shifting Atlantic
circulation patterns, and increased nutrient runoff from deforestation
and intensive agriculture in West Africa and the Amazon basin.

When Sargassum washes ashore in large quantities, it decomposes and
releases hydrogen sulphide and ammonia, gases that are hazardous to
human health at high concentrations. Thick mats of rotting seaweed
make beaches inaccessible for tourism, entangle fishing gear, degrade
water quality, and can damage coral reef ecosystems. For Caribbean
tourism economies, the visual and olfactory impact is particularly
damaging: a beach covered in dark, decomposing seaweed cannot attract
the international visitors that coastal communities depend on.

The Dominican Republic's exposure is acute. Its coastline borders both
the Caribbean Sea and the Atlantic Ocean, and the prevailing currents
and winds bring Sargassum directly to its shores during the peak
season of April through September. While some coastal communities have
developed rudimentary collection and removal operations, the scale of
recent blooms has repeatedly overwhelmed local capacity.

3. DATA

3.1 Satellite Sargassum Data

I measure Sargassum exposure using the Alternative Floating Algae Index
(AFAI), a spectral index derived from NASA's MODIS-Aqua satellite that
detects floating vegetation at the ocean surface. Monthly AFAI data at
4km spatial resolution are sourced from NOAA's ERDDAP data server
(dataset ID: noaa_aoml_atlantic_oceanwatch_AFAI_7D), covering the
waters within 20km of the Dominican coastline from January 2017 to
December 2025.

For each municipality, monthly AFAI values are computed as the mean
across all ocean pixels within the municipality's coastal zone,
excluding nearshore pixels within 3km of the shoreline (which register
persistent positive AFAI due to seagrass and coral bottom reflectance
rather than Sargassum) and inland water bodies including Lago
Enriquillo. Monthly values are then averaged within each calendar
quarter to produce the treatment variable at the municipality-quarter
level. Raw AFAI values are small (full-year mean 0.0008, SD 0.0006, peak-
season SD 0.0006, maximum 0.0033 across the sample); for
interpretability all regression specifications multiply AFAI by 1,000
so that the unit is milli-AFAI (×10⁻³). A one-unit change in the
scaled variable corresponds to a 0.001 increase in raw AFAI, equivalent
to roughly one-third of the observed peak-season range.

Municipalities are classified as coastal (45 of 86 in the analysis
sample) if their administrative boundaries directly touch the ocean
shoreline, excluding the Dominican Republic–Haiti land border.
Non-coastal municipalities are assigned Sargassum exposure using
inverse-distance weighting (IDW) from all coastal municipalities,
with weights proportional to 1/distance². This gives each inland
municipality a geographically smooth exposure value that reflects
the broader regional Sargassum environment rather than a single
nearest neighbour. One municipality, Pepillo Salcedo, is coastal but
its satellite data are contaminated by the Río Masacre river plume;
it is treated as non-coastal and receives IDW-weighted values.

3.2 Household Survey Data

Income and employment data come from the ENCFT, the Dominican
Republic's quarterly continuous labour force survey, using waves from
2017 to 2025. The ENCFT is a repeated cross-section: different
households are interviewed each quarter, so household income cannot
be tracked over time. The natural unit of observation is therefore
the municipality-quarter, which is the level at which the survey is
designed to be representative.

For each municipality-quarter cell I construct three outcome variables:
mean log household income, mean log household income separately for
households in the bottom, middle, and top thirds of the national
per-capita income distribution (log_income_t1, log_income_t2,
log_income_t3), and the mean employment rate. Tertile cutoffs are
computed nationally within each year so that T1 represents the poorest
third of Dominican households, T2 the middle third, and T3 the
wealthiest third. Approximately 17% of surveyed households report zero
income; these fall into T1 and are included in all main specifications.

3.3 Analysis Panel

The final panel is constructed by merging quarterly satellite data
with the quarterly ENCFT municipality aggregates by municipality code
and year-quarter. It contains 3,095 municipality-quarter observations
across 86 municipalities and the years 2017–2025. The peak season
subsample (Q2 and Q3) contains 1,548 observations.

4. EMPIRICAL STRATEGY

4.1 Unit of Observation

The unit of observation is the municipality-quarter. This choice
reflects the structure of the underlying data. Because the ENCFT is
a repeated cross-section, household income is only measurable at the
municipality-quarter level — the mean among the households surveyed
in municipality i during quarter t. Satellite Sargassum intensity is
similarly aggregated to the municipality-quarter by averaging monthly
AFAI values within each quarter. Both the outcome and the treatment
therefore vary at the same level, avoiding any measurement mismatch.

4.2 Estimating Equations

The main estimating equation is:

    log(income_it) = β · AFAI_it + α_i + γ_t + ε_it         (1)

where i indexes municipalities and t indexes year-quarter periods.
AFAI_it is the mean Sargassum intensity in municipality i during
quarter t. α_i are municipality fixed effects and γ_t are year-by-
quarter fixed effects. Standard errors are clustered at the
municipality level.

The municipality fixed effects α_i absorb all time-invariant
differences across municipalities — permanent income levels, geographic
location, distance from major tourism centres, and baseline economic
structure. The year-by-quarter fixed effects γ_t absorb every shock
that affects all municipalities simultaneously in a given quarter of
a given year, including national economic trends, the seasonal pattern
of Sargassum itself, and common weather events. After controlling for
both sets of fixed effects, identification relies entirely on
within-municipality variation in Sargassum intensity across quarters
— that is, quarters in which a specific municipality experienced more
Sargassum than was typical for that place in that season.

To test whether the effect is concentrated during the quarters when
Sargassum blooms are most intense, I estimate equation (1) on a peak
season subsample restricted to Q2 and Q3 (April–September):

    log(income_it) = β_peak · AFAI_it + α_i + γ_t + ε_it    (2)

This subsample captures the core of the Caribbean Sargassum season,
when AFAI values are on average more than twice those recorded in Q1
and Q4.

To test for heterogeneous effects by income group, I replace the
dependent variable with mean log income for households in tertile
group k within the municipality-quarter cell:

    log(income_Tk_it) = β_k · AFAI_it + α_i + γ_t + ε_it    (3)

where k ∈ {bottom, middle, top}. The prediction is that poorer
households — who have fewer savings and less ability to absorb shocks
— should show larger negative coefficients.

I also estimate equations (1) and (2) separately for coastal and
non-coastal municipalities, and jointly with a coastal–non-coastal
interaction term, to test whether direct shoreline exposure produces
a different effect than inland spillovers.

4.3 Identifying Assumption

The key identifying assumption is that, conditional on municipality
and year-by-quarter fixed effects, no unobserved factor simultaneously
drives Sargassum intensity in a specific municipality and that
municipality's mean household income within the same quarter. The most
plausible threat would be a concurrent local shock — such as a
hurricane or drought — that damages both coastal waters and local
incomes. I cannot formally rule this out, which is a limitation of
the analysis.

5. RESULTS

5.1 Main Results

Table 0 shows the fixed effects progression. The OLS coefficient
without any fixed effects is near zero and insignificant. Adding
municipality fixed effects alone produces a large positive coefficient
(+0.216, p < 0.001), reflecting that within-municipality Sargassum
variation correlates positively with income — likely because the
municipalities with the most Sargassum are active coastal areas that
are also relatively prosperous. Adding year-by-quarter fixed effects
alone instead produces a negative coefficient (−0.088, p < 0.05),
showing that time controls are what drive the sign in the expected
direction by removing the seasonal income trends that coincide with
Sargassum season. The two-way specification, which absorbs both sets
of confounders, yields −0.051. This specification is used throughout.

Table 1 presents the main estimates. All coefficients are reported for
AFAI scaled by 1,000 (milli-AFAI units); a one-unit change in the
regressor corresponds to a 0.001 increase in raw AFAI. The full-year
coefficient is -0.051 (SE = 0.035, p = 0.147) — negative in sign but
statistically insignificant. Averaged across all four quarters,
including Q1 and Q4 when Sargassum is largely absent, the signal is
diluted by periods in which treatment is near zero.

The peak season result is markedly sharper. Restricting the sample to
Q2 and Q3, when Sargassum blooms are most intense, yields a coefficient
of -0.080 (SE = 0.039, p = 0.042), significant at the 5% level. A
one-unit increase in milli-AFAI corresponds to a 7.6% reduction in mean
municipal household income (e^−0.080 − 1 = −7.6%). Scaled to one
standard deviation of peak-season Sargassum intensity (0.61 milli-AFAI
units) — a more typical unit of variation — the implied effect is a
0.049 log-point (approximately 4.7%) income reduction. The contrast
between the full-year and peak-season estimates is consistent with
Sargassum being the operative mechanism: the income loss materialises
specifically in the quarters when blooms are present, not year-round.

The employment rate shows no significant response to Sargassum intensity
in either the full-year (-0.004, SE = 0.007, p = 0.582) or peak season
specifications. The absence of an employment effect alongside the
presence of an income effect suggests the channel is through earnings
conditional on employment — reduced hours, lower tip income, seasonal
wage cuts in tourism and hospitality — rather than outright job losses.

5.2 Heterogeneity by Coastal Proximity

Table 4 presents results for coastal and non-coastal municipalities
separately. Over the full year, neither group shows a significant
effect: the coastal coefficient is -0.060 (SE = 0.045, p = 0.183)
and the non-coastal coefficient is -0.028 (SE = 0.051, p = 0.580).

During peak season, the pattern shifts substantially. Coastal
municipalities show a negative but imprecise estimate of -0.055
(SE = 0.044, p = 0.217). Non-coastal municipalities, by contrast,
produce a coefficient of -0.135 (SE = 0.072, p = 0.066) — more than
twice the coastal magnitude, approaching conventional significance.
Table 5 presents the pooled interaction specification; the interaction
coefficient on AFAI × non-coastal is positive and partially offsetting
during the full year, but in the peak season the non-coastal penalty
is large and the interaction is difficult to estimate precisely given
the sample size.

This pattern is initially counterintuitive — one might expect direct
shoreline exposure to produce the largest impacts. Several explanations
are plausible. Coastal households tend to have more diversified income
sources, combining fishing, tourism employment, and local trade, which
may allow partial offsetting of Sargassum shocks when one income
source is disrupted. Non-coastal municipalities that supply the tourism
sector — accommodation, transport, food production, domestic services
— may face a more concentrated demand-side collapse when Sargassum
closes beaches, with fewer alternative income streams to absorb the
shock. Whatever the mechanism, the finding suggests that the economic
costs of Sargassum extend substantially beyond the municipalities that
physically border the sea.

5.3 Heterogeneity by Income Tertile

Tables 2 and 3 present results by income tertile for the full year
and peak season respectively. Over the full year, coefficients follow
the expected gradient: bottom tertile (-0.130, SE = 0.139, p = 0.353),
middle tertile (-0.023, SE = 0.021, p = 0.276), top tertile (+0.012,
SE = 0.037, p = 0.742). None reach conventional significance. The
bottom tertile estimate is particularly imprecise because a share of
municipality-quarter cells in the lowest income group contain households
reporting zero income; log-transforming these produces a floor that
inflates noise in the dependent variable.

During peak season (Table 3), the gradient follows the same pattern:
bottom tertile (-0.140, SE = 0.200, p = 0.487), middle tertile
(-0.040, SE = 0.025, p = 0.119), top tertile (+0.001, SE = 0.039,
p = 0.974). The middle tertile approaches but does not reach
significance. The top tertile shows essentially no response, consistent
with higher-income households having greater ability to absorb shocks
or income sources more insulated from Sargassum. While the point
estimates for the bottom two groups are consistently negative and
ordered in the expected direction, the analysis lacks the statistical
precision to establish the distributional gradient as significant.

6. CONCLUSION

This paper provides evidence that Sargassum inundation reduces
household income in the Dominican Republic. The main finding is a
peak-season income effect of -0.080 log points per milli-AFAI unit
(p < 0.05), equivalent to a 7.6% income reduction per 0.001 increase
in raw AFAI. Scaled to a one-standard-deviation increase in peak-season
Sargassum intensity (0.61 milli-AFAI), the implied effect is 4.7%.
These estimates are identified through within-municipality, within-time
variation after controlling for municipality and year-by-quarter fixed
effects. No significant effect on employment rates is detected,
suggesting the channel operates through earnings rather than job losses.

Two findings extend beyond the headline result. First, the peak-season
effect is larger — and approaches statistical significance — in
non-coastal municipalities, suggesting that Sargassum's economic damage
propagates inland through disruption to tourism supply chains and
regional labour markets. Policy responses focused exclusively on beach
cleanup and coastal remediation may therefore underestimate the true
geographic scope of the problem. Second, the income loss gradient by
tertile is in the expected direction — larger for poorer households —
but is not statistically significant with the current sample. The
absence of significance in the tertile analysis does not rule out a
distributional effect; it reflects the inherent imprecision of
municipality-level means computed from small quarterly survey samples
within income subgroups.

Limitations of this analysis include the repeated cross-section
structure of the ENCFT, which prevents tracking the same households
over time, the municipality-level aggregation of both treatment and
outcome, and the inability to formally rule out time-varying local
confounders. Future work with finer spatial resolution, longer time
series as the Sargassum problem intensifies, health and nutrition
outcomes, or a valid instrumental variable could strengthen
identification and provide a more complete picture of Sargassum's
welfare consequences in the Caribbean.
