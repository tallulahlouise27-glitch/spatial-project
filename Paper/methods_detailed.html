Methods — Detailed Explanation

Sargassum & Household Welfare in the Dominican Republic

1. Overview of the Research Design

The central question is: does Sargassum seaweed make households in the Dominican Republic poorer? To answer this, we need two things — a measure of how much Sargassum was present in a given place at a given time, and a measure of household income in that place at that time. We then ask whether the months and places with more Sargassum also tend to have lower household income, after accounting for all the other reasons why income might vary.

The core challenge is that Sargassum does not arrive randomly. It tends to hit certain parts of the coastline more than others, and it arrives more in some years than others. If those places were already poorer, or if those years happened to coincide with other economic problems, a simple comparison of high-Sargassum and low-Sargassum municipalities would give a misleading answer. Our empirical strategy is designed to isolate the specific variation in Sargassum intensity that is most credibly caused by the algae itself, rather than by underlying geographic or economic differences.

We do this by building a panel dataset — a dataset that tracks the same municipalities repeatedly over time, every month from 2017 to 2025 — and using statistical controls called fixed effects that remove the confounding factors described above. The result is a dataset with 10,263 municipality-month observations across 100 municipalities.

2. Measuring Sargassum: The Satellite Data

2.1 What the Satellite Measures

We measure Sargassum using satellite imagery from NASA's MODIS-Aqua sensor, downloaded from NASA's ERDDAP data platform. MODIS-Aqua is a satellite that passes over the Earth daily and captures detailed images of the ocean surface. We use a derived measure called the Alternative Floating Algae Index (AFAI), which is a specific combination of light wavelengths that detects floating vegetation — including Sargassum — at the ocean surface. When the AFAI value for a pixel is elevated, it indicates that floating algae are present in that patch of ocean.

The satellite data covers the waters within 20 kilometres of the Dominican coastline, at a spatial resolution of roughly 4 kilometres per pixel. We download monthly data, meaning each observation represents the average conditions across all usable satellite passes in that calendar month.

2.2 Constructing the Sargassum Intensity Variable

Raw AFAI values include some background signal even in the absence of Sargassum — essentially, the ocean surface produces a small amount of noise in the sensor. To focus on actual Sargassum, we subtract a baseline threshold of 0.001 and set any values below this to zero. The resulting variable, which we call afai_sargassum, is computed as:

afai_sargassum = mean( max(AFAI − 0.001, 0) )  

This gives us a single number for each municipality-month that captures both how widely Sargassum was spread (coverage) and how dense the mats were (intensity). A value of zero means no detectable Sargassum was present; a larger value means more Sargassum, either covering more ocean pixels or forming thicker mats, or both.

2.3 Assigning Sargassum to Municipalities

The satellite data gives us measurements at the pixel level across the ocean. We need to assign these to municipalities — administrative units on land — so that we can match them to the household survey data. We do this differently depending on whether a municipality is coastal or not.

For coastal municipalities — those whose administrative boundary directly touches the ocean shoreline — we take the average AFAI value across all ocean pixels within 20km of that municipality's coastline. This directly measures the Sargassum in the water immediately adjacent to the shore.

For non-coastal municipalities — those that do not border the sea — we use the same 20km buffer approach, but the relevant pixels are further offshore. This gives a measure of regional Sargassum conditions in the surrounding area rather than direct shoreline exposure.

3. Classifying Municipalities as Coastal or Non-Coastal

We classify each municipality based on whether its boundary directly touches the ocean coastline. This sounds straightforward, but one complication arose: the Dominican Republic shares a land border with Haiti to the west. When we drew the outer boundary of the Dominican Republic, that boundary included both the ocean coastline and the Haiti land border. If we had used the full outer boundary, municipalities along the Haiti border would have been incorrectly classified as coastal.

To fix this, we downloaded the administrative boundary of Haiti, created a small 2km buffer around it, and subtracted it from the Dominican Republic's outer boundary before classifying municipalities. This left only the genuine ocean coastline, and municipalities that touch only the Haiti border are correctly classified as non-coastal.

After this correction, the analysis sample contains 48 coastal municipalities and 52 non-coastal municipalities. Before the correction, 59 municipalities were classified as coastal — the 11 difference were border municipalities that were incorrectly flagged.

4. The Household Survey Data

4.1 What the ENCFT Measures

Household income and employment data come from the Encuesta Nacional Continua de Fuerza de Trabajo (ENCFT), the Dominican Republic's national labour force and income survey. The ENCFT is conducted quarterly — four times per year — and is designed to be statistically representative at the level of individual municipalities within each quarter. It collects information on each household member's employment status, wages, and self-employment income.

We use ENCFT data from 2016 to 2025, covering up to 143 municipalities. For each municipality in each quarter, we compute the average household income (weighted by each household's expansion factor, which adjusts for how many real-world households each surveyed household represents).

4.2 Why We Use Log Income

Rather than using income in raw Dominican pesos (RD$), we use the natural logarithm of income. There are two reasons for this. First, income distributions are typically highly skewed — a small number of very high-income households can distort averages. Taking the log compresses this skew and makes the variable better behaved statistically. Second, using log income means that regression coefficients can be interpreted as percentage or log-point changes, which are more meaningful than raw peso changes when comparing across municipalities with very different baseline income levels.

4.3 Matching Quarterly Survey Data to Monthly Satellite Data

The ENCFT is collected quarterly, but our satellite data is monthly. This creates a mismatch: we have Sargassum measurements for every individual month, but income measurements only four times per year. We resolve this by assigning the same income figure to all three months within a quarter. For example, if a municipality's Q2 (April–June) income was RD$8,000, we assign RD$8,000 to April, May, and June of that year.

This approach is valid because the ENCFT is designed to measure income at the quarterly level — it is the appropriate temporal unit for that data. The year-by-month fixed effects in our regression (described below) account for the fact that income does not actually vary within quarters in our data, so this mechanical feature does not bias the estimates.

5. Income Tertiles

5.1 How Tertiles Are Defined

Rather than using only average household income, we also test whether Sargassum affects different income groups differently. To do this, we divide households into three groups — or tertiles — based on their position in the national income distribution.

Each year, we rank all surveyed households in the Dominican Republic by their per-capita income (total household income divided by the number of household members). We then split this ranked list into three equal groups:

  • Bottom tertile: the poorest third of households nationally
  • Middle tertile: the middle third
  • Top tertile: the richest third

These cutoff points are recalculated each year to account for changes in the overall income distribution over time. For example, in 2024, a household needed per-capita income above RD$12,000 per month to be in the top tertile; by contrast, the same threshold was only RD$7,000 in 2017.

For each municipality-quarter, we then compute the average income separately for the households in each tertile group. This gives us three income series per municipality — one for each income group — which we use as separate dependent variables in the regression.

5.2 The Zero-Income Problem in the Bottom Tertile

A structural problem arises with the bottom tertile. Approximately 5% of municipality-month observations in the bottom group report an average household income of zero. When we take the logarithm of zero, the result is undefined — mathematically, you cannot take the log of zero. In practice, we use log(income + 1) to handle this, which means zero-income observations produce an outcome of exactly zero in the log-transformed variable.

The problem is that these zero-income observations cannot reflect a Sargassum effect, because the income is already at its floor regardless of how much Sargassum was present. This adds noise to the bottom tertile regression and inflates the standard error, making it much harder to detect a real effect even if one exists. Concretely, the standard deviation of log income in the bottom tertile is 1.93 — more than five times larger than the 0.34 for the middle tertile — precisely because of these zero-income observations pulling the distribution around.

5.3 The Non-Zero Tertile Robustness Check

To address this, we construct a second set of tertile income measures that excludes households reporting zero income before computing the tertile boundaries. Roughly 17.6% of all surveyed households — about one in six — report zero income and are excluded from this analysis.

The key difference is that the tertile cutoffs are recomputed on the remaining positive-income sample. This means the "bottom" group in the non-zero analysis is not the same as the original bottom tertile — it is the bottom third of households who actually earn something. The non-zero bottom tertile therefore represents the lowest-earning workers in the country, rather than a mixture of low earners and non-earners. For example, the bottom/middle income boundary rises from RD$5,000 to RD$7,000 per capita per month in 2024 when zero-income households are excluded.

We run the same regressions on this non-zero sample and report the results alongside the original tertile results. If the original bottom-tertile coefficient was being suppressed by zero-income noise, we would expect the non-zero bottom tertile to produce a more negative and more statistically significant result.

6. The Regression Model

6.1 The Basic Idea

The core of our empirical strategy is a regression that asks: within a given municipality, in the months when there is more Sargassum than usual, is income lower than usual for that municipality? And within a given calendar month and year, do municipalities with more Sargassum than other municipalities in that same month have lower income?

The reason we focus on this specific type of variation — rather than simply comparing high-Sargassum and low-Sargassum places — is that municipalities that have historically had more Sargassum might differ from other municipalities in many ways beyond Sargassum: they might be poorer to begin with, or more remote, or more dependent on fishing. If we compared them directly, we would be conflating the effect of Sargassum with all of these pre-existing differences. Fixed effects let us strip out these differences.

6.2 Municipality Fixed Effects

A municipality fixed effect is a statistical control that absorbs every characteristic of a municipality that does not change over time. Think of it as giving each municipality its own intercept in the regression — its own baseline level of income. This means that when we estimate the Sargassum coefficient, we are comparing a municipality to itself across different months, not comparing one municipality to another.

Municipality fixed effects control for things like: geographic location, baseline wealth, proximity to major cities, historical dependence on fishing or tourism, infrastructure quality, and any other stable feature of a place. After removing these, any remaining association between Sargassum and income reflects within-municipality variation over time, not permanent differences between places.

6.3 Year-by-Month Fixed Effects

A year-by-month fixed effect absorbs every shock that affects all municipalities at the same time in the same calendar month of the same year. For example, if the Dominican economy experienced a national recession in mid-2020 that reduced income everywhere simultaneously, that shock would be captured by the year-by-month fixed effect for each month in 2020. Similarly, Sargassum itself has a seasonal pattern — it tends to be higher in May through September every year — and this common seasonal pattern is absorbed by the year-by-month fixed effects.

After removing both municipality fixed effects and year-by-month fixed effects, the only variation left to identify the Sargassum coefficient is deviations from each municipality's own average in each specific month and year. In other words: did this particular municipality have more Sargassum than its own typical level for this time of year, and if so, was its income lower than its own typical level for this time of year?

6.4 The Main Regression Equation

The main estimating equation is:

log(incomeit) = β · AFAIit + αi + γt + εit (1)
where:
  i = municipality
  t = year × month (e.g. "June 2021")
  AFAIit = Sargassum intensity in municipality i in month t
  αi = municipality fixed effect (one per municipality)
  γt = year-by-month fixed effect (one per calendar month per year)
  β = the coefficient of interest: the income effect of Sargassum
  εit = the residual (everything not explained by the model)

The coefficient β tells us: for a one-unit increase in Sargassum intensity (AFAI), how much does log household income change, within the same municipality over time and controlling for common month-year shocks? If β is negative and statistically significant, it means more Sargassum is associated with lower income in a way that we can distinguish from random noise.

Standard errors — the measure of statistical uncertainty around β — are clustered at the municipality level. This accounts for the fact that income and Sargassum within a municipality are likely correlated across months (a municipality that has a good year tends to have good months throughout), so treating each month as fully independent would understate our uncertainty.

6.5 Why This Is More Credible Than a Simple Correlation

A simple correlation between Sargassum and income — without any fixed effects — would be difficult to interpret because of the confounding factors described above. Our fixed effects approach is stronger because it controls for the two most obvious sources of confounding: permanent differences between municipalities, and time trends that affect the whole country at once.

What remains as a potential concern is a local, time-varying confounder — a shock specific to one municipality in one month that simultaneously affects both Sargassum and income. The most plausible example would be a hurricane hitting a specific municipality, damaging economic activity and possibly also stirring up ocean conditions. We cannot formally rule this out, but it is a more demanding requirement than the confounders that fixed effects already address.

In plain terms: We are not just asking "are places with more Sargassum poorer?" (which could reflect many things unrelated to Sargassum). We are asking "when a specific municipality gets more Sargassum than it usually does in that month, does its income fall?" This is a much more targeted question, and one where Sargassum itself is the most plausible explanation for any systematic pattern.

7. Subgroup Analyses

7.1 Peak Season Analysis

Sargassum blooms are seasonal: they typically arrive and intensify between May and September, driven by ocean current patterns and temperature. If Sargassum is genuinely reducing income, we would expect the effect to be stronger during these peak months when Sargassum is actually present, and weaker or absent during off-peak months when it largely is not.

To test this, we estimate equation (1) on a restricted subsample that includes only months 5 through 9 (May–September):

log(incomeit) = βpeak · AFAIit + αi + γt + εit (2)

The peak-season coefficient βpeak can then be compared to the full-year coefficient β from equation (1). If βpeak is substantially larger in magnitude than β, this provides supporting evidence that Sargassum is the mechanism: the income effect is concentrated exactly in the months when Sargassum is present.

7.2 Coastal vs. Non-Coastal Analysis

We might expect the income effect to be larger in coastal municipalities, which face direct beach inundation, than in non-coastal municipalities, which experience Sargassum only indirectly through its effects on regional economic activity. To test this, we estimate equation (1) separately on the 48 coastal and 52 non-coastal municipalities.

Running separate regressions for each subgroup — rather than one pooled regression — allows the Sargassum effect to differ freely between the groups. We report both the full-year and peak-season versions for each group (equations (1) and (2) estimated on each subsample).

7.3 Income Tertile Analysis

To test whether poorer households are more affected than richer ones, we replace the main dependent variable (average municipal log income) with the average log income of households in each tertile group separately. The estimating equation becomes:

log(incomeTk,it) = βk · AFAIit + αi + γt + εit (3)
where k ∈ {bottom, middle, top}

We estimate this equation separately for each tertile group, giving us three coefficients βbottom, βmiddle, and βtop. The prediction from economic theory is that |βbottom| > |βmiddle| > |βtop| — that is, the income loss is largest for the poorest households and smallest for the richest — because poorer households have fewer savings and less access to credit to buffer environmental shocks.

We estimate equation (3) for both the full year and the peak season, and for both the original tertile sample (including zero-income households) and the non-zero tertile sample (excluding them).

8. What the Results Tell Us and What They Do Not

Our results tell us that there is a systematic, negative association between Sargassum intensity and household income in Dominican municipalities, concentrated in peak bloom months, that cannot be explained by permanent geographic differences between places or by shocks common to all municipalities at the same time. This is consistent with Sargassum causing income losses — particularly through its effect on tourism demand — but it is not formal proof of causation.

What the results do not tell us is the precise mechanism through which income falls. We cannot observe whether the effect operates through fewer tourists visiting beaches, lower revenues in hotels and restaurants, reduced fishing access, or some combination of these channels. The finding that the effect is larger in non-coastal municipalities is consistent with a supply chain story — that disruption to beach tourism ripples inland through the regional economy — but we cannot directly confirm this from the data we have.

The results also do not tell us about the impact on the 17.6% of households that report zero income. By construction, income-based measures cannot detect harm to households that have no formal income to lose. These households may well be among the most affected — through loss of informal earnings, food security, or health — but measuring those outcomes would require different data than we have here.
