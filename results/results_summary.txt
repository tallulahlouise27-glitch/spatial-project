INTERPRETATION:
The full-year income coefficient is negative (-28.15) indicating that higher
Sargassum intensity is associated with lower household income, though the
estimate does not reach conventional significance levels (p = 0.13).

The employment rate coefficient is also negative (-4.44) and insignificant,
suggesting Sargassum may reduce employment as well as income, but the data
cannot distinguish this from noise over the full year.

The peak season result (May-Sep, when Sargassum is most intense) is the
strongest finding in the paper: a coefficient of -73.90 significant at 1%.
A one-unit increase in mean excess AFAI during peak months is associated with
a 73.9-log-point reduction in municipal income. This concentration of the
effect in peak months is consistent with Sargassum being the mechanism:
the harm materialises when blooms are actually present.

INTERPRETATION:
The coefficients follow the expected gradient: the poorest households show
the largest negative effect (-144.87), shrinking monotonically through the
middle (-30.75) to the richest third (-12.20). This pattern is consistent
with poorer households being least able to insure against environmental shocks.

Only T2 reaches significance (p = 0.017). The T1 estimate is imprecise for a
structural reason: approximately 5% of municipality-month observations in the
bottom tertile report zero income, producing a log-income outcome of zero
regardless of Sargassum intensity. This floors the dependent variable and
inflates noise (SD of log_income_t1 = 1.93 vs 0.34 for T2). The T1 effect
is likely attenuated — the true impact on the poorest households may be
larger than estimated but is undetectable through the income measure alone.

INTERPRETATION:
Both coastal and not-coastal municipalities show negative effects, but neither
reaches significance over the full year. Coastal municipalities show a larger

is not statistically significant (Wald p = 0.154), meaning we cannot reject
that the two groups experience the same effect size.

INTERPRETATION:
The most striking result in the paper: during peak Sargassum months, the
significant effect is found in NOT-COASTAL municipalities (-84.95, p = 0.001),
while coastal municipalities show a similar magnitude but insignificant
estimate (-76.30, p = 0.156).

This is counterintuitive at first glance but has plausible explanations:
  1. Coastal households may have diversified income (fishing + tourism + trade)
     that allows partial offsetting of Sargassum shocks.
  2. Inland/near-coastal municipalities may depend heavily on tourism supply
     chains, processing, or distribution networks that collapse when Sargassum
     closes beaches, with fewer alternative income sources to fall back on.
  3. Coastal households may have higher income variance year-round (from
     fishing), making it harder to isolate the Sargassum signal statistically.

This finding highlights that Sargassum's economic reach extends beyond the
coastline itself, likely through disruption of tourism and related industries.

INTERPRETATION:
Neither formal interaction test rejects the null of equal effects:
  - Coastal vs not-coastal: Wald p = 0.154
  - Tertile heterogeneity:  Wald p = 0.334

The failure to reject does not mean the effects are identical — the point
estimates follow the expected pattern in both cases. The tests lack power
because (a) the sample has only 100 municipalities and (b) the treatment
variable is noisy. These results should be interpreted as consistent with
the hypothesised heterogeneity, but insufficient to confirm it statistically.

================================================================
SUMMARY OF KEY FINDINGS
================================================================
1. MAIN EFFECT: Sargassum reduces household income, primarily during peak
   season (May-Sep). The peak-season coefficient is -73.90 (p < 0.01),
   concentrated in months when Sargassum blooms are actually present.

2. SPATIAL REACH: The income effect is significant in NOT-COASTAL municipalities
   during peak season (-84.95, p < 0.01), suggesting Sargassum disrupts
   tourism supply chains and regional economies beyond the shoreline.

3. DISTRIBUTIONAL EFFECTS: The negative effect is largest for the poorest
   households (T1: -145, T2: -31, T3: -12 full year), consistent with
   limited insurance capacity among low-income households. Only T2 reaches
   significance; T1 is attenuated by zero-income reporting.

4. IDENTIFICATION: All models include municipality and year×month fixed effects,
   removing time-invariant geographic differences and all common national shocks.
   Identification relies on within-municipality monthly variation in Sargassum.
   Remaining concern: local time-varying confounders (e.g. concurrent fishing
   shocks) cannot be ruled out without a valid instrument.
