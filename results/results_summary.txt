INTERPRETATION:
The full-year income coefficient is small and negative (-5.71) but far from
significant, indicating no detectable average effect across all months.

The employment rate coefficient is also negative (-1.86) and insignificant.

The peak season result (May-Sep, when Sargassum is most intense) is the
strongest finding in the paper: a coefficient of -64.69 significant at 5%.
A one-unit increase in mean excess AFAI during peak months is associated with
a 64.7-log-point reduction in municipal income. This concentration of the
effect in peak months is consistent with Sargassum being the mechanism:
the harm materialises when blooms are actually present.

INTERPRETATION:
The coefficients follow the expected gradient: the poorest households show
the largest negative effect (-157.9), shrinking monotonically through the
middle (-20.62) to the richest third (-11.04). This pattern is consistent
with poorer households being least able to insure against environmental shocks.

None of the three groups reach conventional significance over the full year.
The T1 estimate is imprecise because approximately 5% of municipality-month
observations in the bottom tertile report zero income, producing a log-income
outcome of zero regardless of Sargassum intensity. This floors the dependent
variable and inflates noise (SD of log_income_t1 = 1.93 vs 0.34 for T2).
The T1 effect is likely attenuated — the true impact on the poorest households
may be larger than estimated but is undetectable through the income measure alone.

INTERPRETATION:
During peak Sargassum months, the significant effect is found in NOT-COASTAL
municipalities (-70.87, p < 0.05), while coastal municipalities show a negative
but insignificant estimate (-58.95).

This is counterintuitive at first glance but has plausible explanations:
  1. Coastal households may have diversified income (fishing + tourism + trade)
     that allows partial offsetting of Sargassum shocks.
  2. Inland/near-coastal municipalities may depend heavily on tourism supply
     chains, processing, or distribution networks that collapse when Sargassum
     closes beaches, with fewer alternative income sources to fall back on.
  3. Coastal households may have higher income variance year-round (from
     fishing), making it harder to isolate the Sargassum signal statistically.

This finding highlights that Sargassum's economic reach extends beyond the
coastline itself, likely through disruption of tourism and related industries.

INTERPRETATION:
Neither formal interaction test rejects the null of equal effects:
  - Coastal vs not-coastal: Wald p = 0.658
  - Tertile heterogeneity:  Wald p = 0.459

The failure to reject does not mean the effects are identical — the point
estimates follow the expected pattern in both cases. The tests lack power
because (a) the sample has only 100 municipalities and (b) the treatment
variable is noisy. These results should be interpreted as consistent with
the hypothesised heterogeneity, but insufficient to confirm it statistically.

INTERPRETATION:
Table 7 replicates Tables 2 and 5 after excluding households reporting zero income.
The zero-income households in the original T1 group floor the log-income outcome
at zero regardless of Sargassum intensity, inflating measurement noise and
attenuating the estimated coefficient.

By removing these households and recomputing tertile cutoffs on the positive-income
sample, T1_nz represents the lowest-earning workers (not the non-earners). The
key question is whether the T1_nz coefficient is more precisely estimated and
whether the gradient across tertiles sharpens relative to Tables 2 and 5.

Comparison with original tertile results (Table 2 / Table 5):
  - If T1_nz is more negative and/or more significant than original T1,
    this confirms the zero-income floor was masking the effect on the poorest earners.
  - If coefficients are similar across the two samples, the zero-income households
    had little influence on the point estimates (effect operates mainly through
    income variance, not the zero floor).

================================================================
SUMMARY OF KEY FINDINGS
================================================================
1. MAIN EFFECT: Sargassum reduces household income during peak season (May-Sep).
   The peak-season coefficient is -64.69 (p < 0.05), concentrated in months
   when Sargassum blooms are actually present.

2. SPATIAL REACH: The income effect is significant in NOT-COASTAL municipalities
   during peak season (-70.87, p < 0.05), suggesting Sargassum disrupts
   tourism supply chains and regional economies beyond the shoreline.

3. DISTRIBUTIONAL EFFECTS: The negative effect is largest for the poorest
   households (T1: -158, T2: -21, T3: -11 full year), consistent with
   limited insurance capacity among low-income households. None reach
   significance over the full year; T2 is significant in peak season.
   Table 7 reports the same analysis excluding zero-income households for robustness.

4. IDENTIFICATION: All models include municipality and year×month fixed effects,
   removing time-invariant geographic differences and all common national shocks.
   Identification relies on within-municipality monthly variation in Sargassum.
   Remaining concern: local time-varying confounders (e.g. concurrent fishing
   shocks) cannot be ruled out without a valid instrument.
